WatchNext

What makes audiences love a movie — and if people love one movie, what else might they love?

A movie can earn a high rating without attracting much attention. Two films from different genres can appeal to the same audience, while familiar actor–director partnerships connect entire groups of movies. WatchNext combines MovieLens audience ratings with TMDB metadata and reviews to explore what a single rating leaves out.

      Insight — why it matters

      Finding a meaningful next movie takes more than one score

      WatchNext shows why one rating cannot fully describe a movie. Genre performance changes when we compare ratings with voting activity. Movies liked by similar audiences form neighborhoods shaped by both era and genre. Attention, numerical ratings, and written reactions often disagree. Behind those patterns, repeated actor–director partnerships create recognizable creative communities.

      1. 1 The metric changes the story. Animation is the highest-rated genre, but Action draws the most votes.
      2. 2 Audience similarity has structure. Movies liked by the same people usually share a release decade and at least one genre.
      3. 3 Acclaim is not attention. Ratings, discussion, and review tone capture different audience responses.
      4. 4 Movies are also social products. Recurring actor–director partnerships form visible creative communities.

      How to find your next movie

      1. Start with a movie you love and find it in the Movie Universe.
      2. Open its audience neighborhood to see what its viewers also liked.
      3. Check those neighbors in the scatterplot: are they acclaimed, or simply widely discussed?
      4. Follow the people behind it in the collaboration network and career view.

      The result is not a single automatic recommendation, but a more transparent way to understand why a movie may be worth watching.

      Limitations

      • The dataset favors popular titles with many MovieLens ratings or TMDB votes, so it does not represent every released film.
      • MovieLens ratings end in October 2023, so the newest movies have no audience-overlap data and are placed by content only.
      • TMDB vote count measures voting activity, not complete popularity or discussion.
      • Review samples are small for many movies (median 3 reviews), and the sentiment model may read dark subject matter as negative.
      • Box-office revenue is missing for many streaming-first releases, which therefore do not appear in the scatterplot.
      • The collaboration network only includes people with at least five movies and pairs with at least three shared films.

      Appendix

      Data and methods

      Raw datasets

      • MovieLens 32M — 32,000,204 ratings and 2,000,072 free-text tags from 200,948 users, across 87,585 movies (January 1995 – 12 October 2023). grouplens.org
      • TMDB API — movie metadata (genres, budget, revenue, ratings), cast and crew credits, keywords, and user reviews, pulled per movie via the TMDB API. developer.themoviedb.org

      Processed dataset

      2,914 movies, built in two tracks and merged:

      • Historical (1,998 movies, 1922–2022) — MovieLens movies with a TMDB ID and at least 50 ratings; the top 2,000 by rating count were selected, and two whose TMDB entries no longer exist were dropped. Each has real MovieLens user-rating data, which the audience similarity in Chapter 2 needs. This track leans toward the 1990s and 2000s, the era MovieLens users rated most.
      • Recent (916 movies, 2016–2026) — MovieLens ratings stop in October 2023 and thin out well before that for newer releases, so this track is discovered directly from TMDB, sorted by vote count. Vote count is stable while paging; TMDB's popularity score changes continuously and would produce duplicates and gaps.

      Each movie carries TMDB metadata, its top 10 billed cast, director(s), and TMDB keywords (28,991 cast credits, 3,173 director credits, 47,201 keywords). 11,481 TMDB reviews covering 2,648 of the 2,914 movies were cleaned, deduplicated, and scored for sentiment.

      Data cleaning and processing

      • Parsed release year and genre list from MovieLens's raw title and genre strings, and normalized IMDb IDs as zero-padded strings, since leading zeros are significant.
      • Validated MovieLens ratings (dropped values outside 0.5–5 or off the half-star scale, and duplicate user–movie pairs) and converted timestamps to dates.
      • Removed movies that appeared in both tracks, keeping the MovieLens-linked copy, and treated budget or revenue values of zero or above $3 billion as missing, since these crowd-edited fields occasionally carry placeholder numbers.
      • Cleaned review text (stripped HTML and URLs, dropped reviews under 20 characters and exact duplicates) and scored sentiment with siebert/sentiment-roberta-large-english. We chose it over a tweet-tuned model because our reviews run about 170 words at the median.
      • Extracted the top 8 review keywords per movie with TF–IDF over unigrams and bigrams.

      Measures used in the charts

      • Weighted rating (Chapter 1): the average TMDB rating of a genre–year cell, weighted by each movie's vote count.
      • Audience similarity (Chapter 2): the Jaccard overlap between the sets of users who rated two movies at least 4 out of 5.
      • Discussion (Chapter 3): a movie's TMDB vote-count percentile among movies released within ±2 years.
      • Review tone (Chapter 3): the share of positive reviews, adjusted toward the overall average for movies with few reviews.
      • Collaboration strength (Chapter 4): the number of movies an actor and a director made together.
